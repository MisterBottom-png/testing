A story that lasts 0.17 seconds

OneDrumHit

A stick comes down. A sixth of a second later, you feel it. A lot happens in between.

Scroll to slow time down. Tap the drum.

Hit

0 ms

The stick touches the skin. It stays there for about 5 thousandths of a second. That is all the time it gets.

2 ms

The skin does not shake as one piece. It breaks into shapes. Some parts rise while others fall, and the lines between them stay still. Sand would gather on those lines.

4 ms

Each shape hums its own note. On a string the notes line up neatly. On a drum they do not: 1, then 1.59, then 2.14, then 2.30. That messy stack is why a drum sounds like a drum.

Air

5 ms

The skin shoves the air next to it. That air shoves the air next to it. Nothing travels to you except the shove.

7 ms

The shove moves at 343 metres a second. That is 1,235 km/h, faster than a passenger jet.

10 ms

When the stick lifted off, the first shove was already 1.7 metres away. You are standing 3.4 metres from the drum. It reaches you now.

Ear

10 ms

The air taps your eardrum. Behind it, three tiny bones pass the tap along. The last one is the smallest bone in your body, about 3 millimetres long.

11 ms

The bones knock on a tube curled up like a snail shell. Rolled out flat, it would be about 34 millimetres long.

14 ms

High sounds stop near the door. Low sounds roll all the way to the middle. The crack of the stick and the boom of the drum land in different places.

Hairs

15 ms

Along the tube stand about 15,000 hair cells. One row listens. Three rows help.

16 ms

When the wave passes, the hairs bend. Bending opens tiny gates, and the cell fires. Right here, a sound stops being movement and becomes electricity.

17 ms

You were born with every hair cell you will ever have. They do not grow back. Loud drums are worth earplugs.

Brain

17 ms

About 30,000 nerve fibres carry the signal out of your ear.

20 ms

It jumps through a chain of stations deep in your head, each one a few thousandths of a second after the last.

30 ms

The first cells in the hearing part of your brain fire. You still do not know you heard anything.

Room

30 ms

Meanwhile, the same sound has been bouncing off the walls. Those copies took the long way round, so they turn up late.

60 ms

You do not hear them as echoes. Your brain glues them onto the first sound, and uses the delay to feel how big the room is.

You

110 ms

A big wave of activity sweeps the hearing part of your brain. This is about when you notice the sound.

170 ms

Now you can answer it. Nod. Tap a foot. Hit the next note.

Nobody can fully explain the last step, where a shove of air turns into a feeling. But you just had one.

That was all of it. 0.17 seconds.
